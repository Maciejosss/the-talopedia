The **Nawame period** (縄目時代, *Nawame jidai*) covers the prehistory of [[metropolitan-nichirin]] from approximately 14,000 to 800 BCE, named for cord-marked pottery (縄目土器, *nawame doki*) prominent at the time. It follows the Nichirian Paleolithic and precedes the [[hinode-period|Hinode period]], whose onset is marked by the appearance of bronze, iron, and wet-rice cultivation. The period is divided into six subperiods: Incipient, Initial, Early, Middle, Late, and Final.

As of 1934, approximately 9,000 sites from the Nawame period had been registered with the Office of Historical Documentation. Contemporaneous assemblages in [[tohanto]] and [[manshu]] are assigned to separate traditions and are known from fewer sites, as many of those in Tohanto were destroyed during the [[pacification-of-tohanto]] and many of those in Manshu during [[the-koin-war|the Kōin War]]. Nawame material has also been recovered on the coast of [[hyperborea]], where it is the earliest known evidence of human presence.

## Chronology

Shell mounds in Metropolitan Nichirin were recorded by antiquarians of the [[miyamoto-period|Miyamoto period]], who collected ground stone axes from them as “thunder axes” (雷斧, *raifu*), but were not systematically excavated before the 20th century. The period was conceived with the uncovering of the Kaigarazaka shell mound in [[shinkeisei]] during the construction of the Shinkeisei Metro between 1917 and 1923.

From 1919 to 1921, Professor Tokuzō Kirihara of the Kugayama Institute of Historical Studies at [[kyowa-university|Kyōwa University]] directed salvage excavations at the site. Over a depth of 6.2 meters, the excavations recovered a stratified sequence of pottery, lithics, faunal remains, and 41 burials. Kirihara introduced the term *nawame doki* in his report of 1922. Between 1923 and 1931, he and his students used the stratigraphy of Kaigarazaka as the basis of a seriation of pottery styles, known as the Kugayama sequence, which remains the standard chronology of the period.

Absolute dates are derived by correlating the tephra layers from eruptions of the [[greater-nichirian-alps]] with the annual sediment laminae (varves) in cores drawn from [[lake-ryusei|Lake Ryūsei]]. The principal marker horizon is the Ko-Sōten Yellow Pumice (KYP), dated by varve count to approximately 5,500 BCE, produced by the collapse of Ko-Sōten (古蒼天, “Old Sōten”), the edifice that preceded the present cone of [[mount-soten|Mount Sōten]]. Its pyroclastic deposits reach a thickness of 20 meters on the flanks of the volcano and 1.5 meters at a distance of 60 kilometers. Across the central coastal plain, sites beneath the KYP yield only Initial pottery, while those above it, reoccupied after a hiatus of approximately three centuries, yield Early pottery. The eruption is accordingly taken as the boundary between the two subperiods.

The record is incomplete due to earthquakes, river flooding, and tsunamis, which removed or redeposited many coastal sites, and the acidic volcanic soils of the uplands' lack of preservative capacity. Shell middens are the principal exception, as the calcium carbonate of discarded shell neutralizes the surrounding soil. They account for nearly all human and faunal remains recovered from the period.

## Material culture

### Pottery

Nawame potters built their vessels from coils of clay and decorated them by rolling twisted cords across the wet surface. The small round-bottomed bowls of the Incipient subperiod gave way to pointed-bottomed jars, which were set directly into hearth ash, and these in turn to the large flat-bottomed storage jars of the Middle subperiod. Carbonized residues show that the vessels were used to boil acorns, chestnuts, shellfish, and fish. The Kugayama sequence distinguishes subperiods by the direction and number of the cord twists, of which more than thirty types have been identified in Middle assemblages from Kaigarazaka.

### Lithics

From the Early subperiod onward, obsidian quarried on the offshore islands, identifiable by its inclusions, reached mainland sites up to 400 kilometers from its source, supplementing the andesite and slate worked locally. By the Middle and Late subperiods, jadeite beads and pendants circulated on both sides of the Tohanto Isthmus.

### Settlements

Pit dwellings first appeared in the Initial subperiod, in clusters of three to ten. By the Middle subperiod, the largest settlements had grown into ring villages of up to a hundred dwellings arranged around a central plaza and burial ground. Their inhabitants cultivated barnyard millet and soybeans and managed stands of chestnut. At Ushiozaki, near [[genkai]], a Middle village of fourteen dwellings lies beneath a tsunami deposit dated to approximately 2,800 BCE.

### Burial and ritual

Threshold burial, the interment of infants and young children in jars beneath the entrances of dwellings, is recorded in 312 instances at 47 sites. Older members of Middle and Late villages were buried in the central plaza. Communities also deposited whole vessels, polished axes, and red-pigmented stones at river fords. At [[futagawa]], the largest of the eleven such deposits recovered on the rivers of the northern seaboard, the deposit held 214 vessels.

## Hyperborean sites

In 1931, researchers from [[forward-operating-base-keter]] collected cord-marked sherds from a shell midden on a raised beach of the coastal plain. The material, published by Kirihara in 1933, comprises 63 sherds of Early type, 2 obsidian blades, and the faunal remains of seal, walrus, and seabirds. The obsidian has been matched to the island sources that supplied mainland Early assemblages. No dwellings were identified, and the midden has been interpreted as a seasonal sealing camp. Kirihara proposed that voyages to Hyperborea began after the Ko-Sōten eruption had displaced the population of the central coastal plain.

## Population and origins

Skeletal series from Kaigarazaka and other middens describe a population shorter in stature than modern Nichirians, with a mean adult male stature estimated at 158 centimeters. The skulls show broad faces, prominent brow ridges, and severe dental attrition, attributed to a diet of shellfish and stone-ground nuts.

Anatomist Ryōhei Tsuge of the University of Shinkeisei first proposed in 1897 that the builders of the shell mounds had been displaced from the coastal plains by later migrants, the ancestors of the modern Nichirians. After the Nawame period was defined by Kirihara, Tsuge’s successors in the school of replacement identified the Nawame with the ancestors of the Gilyaks and other indigenous peoples of Sangai. They cite shared cranial traits and the cord-impressed decoration of Gilyak basketry..

The school of continuity, associated with Kirihara and the Kugayama Institute, holds that the Nawame population formed the principal ancestry of the Nichirians and absorbed the Hinode migrants over several centuries. It draws on cranial measurements published by Takarazuka Imperial University in 1926, which placed Hinode-period burials between the two populations. Kaiseido scholarship has adopted the continuity account, reading threshold burials and ford deposits as early forms of the worship of the kami of doorways and river crossings. It is also the account presented in textbooks approved by the Ministry of Education.
